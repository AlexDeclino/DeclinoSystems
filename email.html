Session #000  ·  April 23, 2026

Sybil is a research & development centre for weird gaming. We focus our residency and cultural program on games that sieves through the cracks between the art world and the AAA industry, becoming insidious intrusions of the outside into the familiar. For us gaming is a superstructure: a cauldron capable of incorporating visual art, protocol design, music, narrative, architecture, economics, and social simulations within a single coherent system. Far from being mere entertainment, we believe weird gaming is where the most important questions about play and new technologies will be worked out.
In Remote Viewing — our newsletter dropped on a variable reward schedule — you will find Sybil's internal events program, a curated selection of essays about game theory and design and first access to our residents projects and their recommendations.

In this remote viewing session:

Alex recommends What's the point if we can't have fun by David Graeber, Arcade classics spawn art? by Tiffany Holmes and a new album by Fcuckers
Joaquina recommends Otaku: Water levels. While building the mechanics and searching for sounds that feel like an adrenalized dreamscape, some of these tracks found their way into the making of her game.
Beth recommends Case Volume One by Klein
Danielle recommends Darkwood, a slow top down horror that gives you insight into how limiting what the player knows and sees is all you need to build a terrifying world and a sense of dread
Jira Has been thinking a lot about the 2019 game Wattam by Keita Takahashi (creator of Katamari Damacy). In an age of standardised game genres and interfaces Wattam demonstrates the way the player actually plays the game is perhaps the most important thing. You freely float from character to character, holding hands, flying like a deflating balloon and just causing chaotic mischief without any clear game loop. It's the closest I've seen a game achieve the pure unstructured play of children.
dmstfctn recommends Rehearsals for Retirement. They also have been working on Trial Trial, a live, role-played court hearing (LARP) in which a contemporary dual-use technology — one used in both civilian and military contexts— is put on trial as if it were legally responsible for harm.
