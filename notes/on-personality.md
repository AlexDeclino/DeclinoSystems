On Personality, Honesty, Disney and Descent (1994)
Jun 6, 2026

**Descent (1994)** dropped players into a 3D labyrinth with no fixed floor, no gravity, no reliable sense of which way was up. The player’s conventional, sense of up and down vanished and was replaced with a totally novel and highly disorienting way of moving through the game space. To help with navigation, the developers added a simple robot companion called Guidebot. It had one job: it knew where you needed to go. It would dart ahead, then come back, blinking to follow it. After the chaos of a battle left you spinning and directionless, that little blinking light had an almost unreasonable calming power. Guidebot became an extension of the player’s sensory perceptions, a kind of phenomenological augmentation, a new way of situating yourself in the world.
There are far more sophisticated game characters since then, ones with dialogue trees, emotional arcs, behavioral complexity that Guidebot couldn't dream of. But what made Guidebot stick over them were two things: honesty and personality.  

**On honesty:** Guidebot wasn’t pretending to be a person or a dog or a magical creature. If you could, in the game, open up Guidebot and examine the code that defined it, you would see the same actual code that defined Guidebot the game object. It feels a bit like the brushstrokes in a Cezanne painting when they stop trying to be trees or clouds or mountains and start to become what they are, brushstrokes on a canvas. maybe Guidebot hints at a similar future for LLMs, one in which they offer us a chance to engage directly with intelligent systems, not as simulations, but as themselves. 

**On personality**: Guidebot felt real and alive because although simple it had personality. The illusion of life. 
Like Disney says: “for a character to feel real it must have a personality”. The search for complexity or life-like accuracy is futile. All it matters is personality. Disney animator actually discarded complexity, in order to achieve the core emotiveness of a character, that’s the only important thing to make the character feel like it has a life on its own. They were building not animated characters, but animated personalities. Perhaps that’s what we are making, new personalities. Here feelings are more relevant than intellect (of the creature). Disney illusion of life came from new ways of thinking about animation, new ways of drawing, and relate drawings to each other. Disney animated personalities were born thanks to the company’s technological innovations. At first the cartoon medium was flat, people were into it because it was a novelty (rubber hose era). It never really became popular until they developed the medium beyond the tricks and illusions, until they began developing personalities. *We are in the rubber hose era of AI characters.*
